## Source / Context

This case documents Mozilla's 2016–2017 identity redesign with Johnson Banks. It is useful because the public record exposes far more of the decision process than a typical agency case study: diagnosis, brand personality, strategy territories, narrative development, first design routes, refinement, research, selection, and final system.

The project is especially useful for studying **how brand strategy becomes visual direction**, rather than only studying the final `moz://a` identity.

The source material frames the process broadly as:

```text
Ideation -> Concepting -> Refinement -> Guidance
```

For study purposes, a more detailed chain is:

```text
Problem / Diagnosis
-> Brand Personality
-> Strategy Territories
-> Feedback / Correction
-> Narrative Directions
-> Design Routes
-> Selection + Iteration
-> Testing
-> Design Judgment
-> Final Identity System
```

## Why This Case Matters

Typical public case studies compress months of uncertainty into a clean before/after story. Mozilla Open Design preserves much more of the uncertainty: multiple hypotheses, rejected routes, community feedback, research conflict, and the logic used to narrow options.

The useful object of study is therefore not the logo itself, but the **decision chain**.

A practical reading lens for this case is:

```text
Observation
-> Insight
-> Strategic Decision
-> Creative Territory
-> Design Decision
-> Validation
```

## What Happened

### 1. The project started with an identity problem, not a logo problem

Early material focuses on Mozilla's brand reality: fragmented visual expression, confusion between Mozilla and Firefox, and the limits of a conventional top-down branding process for an open organization.

Core reading:

- [Designing in the Open](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/designing-in-the-open/)
- [Branding in a New Land](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/branding-in-a-new-land/)
- [Empowering Open Design by Involving the Community](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/empowering-open-design-by-involving-the-community/)

### 2. Strategy was explored before visual identity

Research and interviews produced provisional brand personality / positioning hypotheses. These were then expanded into seven thematic strategy territories rather than immediately converted into logos.

Core reading:

- [Our Brand Personality](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/our-brand-personality/)
- [Creative Strategy On View](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/creative-strategy-on-view/)

Seven strategy territories:

1. [For All](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/for-all/)
2. [The Guide](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/the-guide/)
3. [The Protector](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/the-protector/)
4. [For Good](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/for-good/)
5. [Change Makers](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/change-makers/)
6. [For Freedom](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/for-freedom/)
7. [The Fight](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/the-fight/)

Useful study question:

```text
Brand hypothesis
-> semantic associations
-> behaviours
-> visual metaphors
```

The boards are most useful when read as **strategic hypotheses and semantic worlds**, not moodboards to imitate aesthetically.

### 3. Feedback changed the framing

The team publicly acknowledged that the initial territories had missed important qualities, especially optimism. The seven territories were explicitly treated as exploration rather than final answers.

Core reading:

- [What we've learned so far…and why we deserve a dope-slap](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/what-weve-learned-so-farand-why-we-deserve-a-dope-slap/)
- [Going deeper into Mozilla's identity problem](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/going-deeper-into-mozillas-identity-problem/)
- [RSVP. Yes](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/rsvp-yes/)

This is an important inflection point because the process does not move linearly from strategy to design. It loops back to diagnosis and reframes the brief.

### 4. Seven territories were synthesized into five narratives

After feedback, the strategic exploration was recombined into five more developed narrative directions.

- [And then there were five](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/and-then-there-were-five/)

This stage sits between abstract brand strategy and visual concepting. It is useful for studying how a broad strategic space becomes a more actionable creative brief.

### 5. The first visible identity round contained seven design routes

Only after the strategy / narrative work did the project move into distinct visual routes.

Overview:

- [Now for the fun part of Mozilla's logo design](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/now-for-the-fun-part/)

First-round routes:

- [A — The Eye](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-a-the-eye/)
- [B — The Connector](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-b-the-connector/)
- [C — The Open Button](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-c-the-open-button/)
- [D — Protocol](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-d-protocol/)
- [E — Wireframe World](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-e-wireframe-world/)
- [F — The Impossible M](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-f-the-impossible-m/)
- [G — Flik Flak](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/design-route-g-flik-flak/)

Follow-up:

- [Now we're talking!](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/now-were-talking/)

The useful comparison is not simply which route looks best, but **which narrative each route translates, what it preserves, and what it loses**.

### 6. Seven routes narrowed to four and were redesigned

The next stage selected four contenders and developed them further rather than treating the first proposals as finished answers.

- [Progress in the Making](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/progress-in-the-making/)
- [Route 1 — Protocol 2.0](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/route-one-protocol-2-0/)
- [Route 2 — Flame](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/round-two-flame/)
- [Route 3 — Burst](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/route-three-burst/)
- [Route 4 — Dino 2.0](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/route-four-dino-2-0/)

This phase is particularly useful for studying **iteration as transformation**, not just polishing.

### 7. Research informed the decision but did not mechanically choose the winner

Later rounds incorporated designer judgment, Mozillian / public feedback, and quantitative consumer testing across multiple markets.

- [Fine Tuning](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/fine-tuning/)
- [Nearly There](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/nearly-there/)

A key tension appeared between Protocol and Burst:

- Protocol carried stronger Mozilla / internet specificity but risked feeling too technical and less human.
- Burst tested as warmer and more accessible but raised the question of whether it was distinctively Mozilla without the name.

The response was not simply to accept whichever route scored highest. The final direction retained Protocol's strategic recognizability while absorbing qualities such as warmth / dimensionality learned from other directions.

Study proposition from this case:

```text
Research does not replace design judgment.
Research informs design judgment.
```

Treat this as a case-supported learning, not yet a universal Principle.

### 8. The system converged into `moz://a`

Final refinement continued through qualitative research and system development before launch.

- [Heading into the Home Stretch](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/heading-into-the-home-stretch/)
- [Arrival](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/arrival/)

The final result should be read last. Reading it first makes the earlier process look inevitable when it was not.

### 9. Post-project material reveals rejected thinking and system depth

- [Roads Not Taken](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/roads-not-taken/)
- [Zilla Slab: A Common Language Through a Shared Font](https://blog.mozilla.org/opendesign/zilla-slab-common-language-shared-font/)

`Roads Not Taken` is especially useful because rejected directions expose the actual search space and the reasons design routes died.

## Key Decision

The most important decision was not the selection of a single logo route. It was the repeated choice to **treat identity design as a sequence of hypotheses that could be reframed, recombined, tested, and judged**, rather than as a straight line from brief to logo.

The later Protocol / Burst tension is the clearest example: research surfaced different strengths for different audiences, and the team used that evidence to refine the strategic design judgment rather than outsourcing the decision to the research score.

## Evidence

The public record includes:

- diagnosis and problem framing;
- provisional personality / positioning work;
- seven strategy territories;
- public feedback and explicit correction of earlier assumptions;
- five narrative directions;
- seven first-round design routes;
- four refined routes;
- quantitative and qualitative testing;
- final selection and system rollout;
- postmortem discussion of rejected routes.

This level of process visibility makes the case unusually strong evidence for studying brand design decision-making.

## Interpretation

### 1. Strategy territories are not style directions

The seven early boards are better understood as competing hypotheses about what Mozilla could mean. Their value is in defining different semantic and behavioural worlds that can later produce different design routes.

### 2. A good process preserves divergence long enough to learn

The process repeatedly expands and contracts:

```text
7 territories
-> 5 narratives
-> 7 design routes
-> 4 refined routes
-> final direction
```

The numbers themselves are not a method to copy. What matters is that divergence is used to expose meaningful alternatives before convergence.

### 3. Iteration can change the idea, not only improve execution

The second-round routes are useful because they show that iteration is not just making a logo cleaner. Feedback can change the concept, balance, symbolism, and system logic.

### 4. Distinctiveness and broad appeal can conflict

The Protocol / Burst result shows a recurring identity problem: the route that feels most immediately accessible may not be the route with the strongest brand-specific meaning.

This is worth connecting to future cases about recognition, distinctive assets, and research-led design decisions.

## Harper's Current Study Focus

The useful goal for this case is not to memorize the final identity or imitate the visual language. It is to reconstruct **how an international branding project moves from diagnosis to design judgment**.

When rereading the source, annotate each stage using:

```text
Observation
-> Insight
-> Strategic Decision
-> Creative Territory
-> Design Decision
-> Validation
```

This makes the case comparable with future brand projects instead of leaving it as an isolated story.

## Transferable Learning

Current case-supported learnings:

- Do not collapse a brand problem into a logo problem too early.
- Creative territories can function as strategic hypotheses before visual routes exist.
- Feedback can legitimately reopen diagnosis instead of only editing execution.
- Selection and iteration should preserve the reason a route is valuable, not merely its appearance.
- Research is one input into identity decisions; it does not automatically substitute for design judgment.
- Rejected directions are valuable evidence because they reveal the boundaries of the final decision.

These remain **case-supported learnings**, not Principles, until they are supported by additional cases or real project experience.
